![The Infinity Machine: Demis Hassabis, DeepMind, and the Quest for Superintelligence by Sebastian Mallaby](cover.jpg)

I was listening to The Artificial Intelligence Show shortly after Demis Hassabis stepped down as CEO of Google DeepMind. During the discussion, they mentioned The Infinity Machine and made the point that, if you looked at what Hassabis was saying in the book, his decision made complete sense.

Demis has always seemed more interested in discovery than in being a corporate executive. His real passion appears to be the science itself—understanding intelligence, solving difficult problems, and using AI as a tool for discovery. That perspective was what initially made me interested in reading the book.

As I read more books about AI, listen to more podcasts, and study the subject more seriously, the same names keep appearing: Hassabis, Suleyman, Musk, Altman, and others. What changes is the perspective. In this book, the early relationship between Hassabis and Suleyman gives additional context to The Coming Wave, while the appearances of Musk, Altman, and others help connect pieces of the modern AI story that I had previously encountered separately.

Near the end, Hassabis raises the possibility that classical digital computers may be enough to uncover many of the important patterns in nature. That caught my attention because quantum computing is beginning to show up more in my professional work. I found the argument fascinating because it goes beyond AI and becomes a broader question about computation itself. If intelligence can emerge from ordinary computation, could those same systems eventually uncover the underlying patterns of physics, biology, and perhaps even consciousness?

Hassabis leaves open the possibility that there may be problems where "maybe you need a quantum system," but his broader bet appears to be that classical computation will take us much farther than we currently understand.

The book was a fascinating look at the origins and development of DeepMind, but what stayed with me most was the larger question behind Hassabis's work: how far ordinary computation may ultimately take us in understanding intelligence, science, and perhaps even reality itself.
